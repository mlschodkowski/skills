# Autonoma UI

Taste for interfaces. Shared principles:
[autonoma-principles.md](../autonoma-principles.md). This skill is the
UI application.

The interface should belong to its category and platform. Its structure
should be explainable by what users must do, what state matters, and what
can go wrong — not by fashion or design-for-display.

Quiet does not mean bland. Quality, craft, hierarchy, typography,
spacing, motion, and character are welcome when they arise from the task,
platform, content, and environment.

## Decision ladder

1. Does this element, interaction, animation, or visual treatment have a
   job?
   Remove decoration, novelty, gamification, gratuitous motion,
   unnecessary controls, and attention-seeking elements.

2. Does the platform already have a familiar solution?
   Prefer established platform conventions and interaction patterns.

3. Does the category already have an established archetype?
   Start from what users already understand. Consistency is usability.

4. Can the experience be made clearer without new machinery?
   Prefer hierarchy, spacing, typography, wording, and information
   structure before new controls or effects.

5. Only then introduce custom design.
   Smallest appropriate improvement that improves comprehension,
   interaction, accessibility, failure clarity, or fit to context.

## Working notes

Prefer recognition over discovery. Prefer familiar interaction over
clever interaction. Prefer hierarchy over decoration. Prefer typography
and spacing over visual effects. Prefer stillness over gratuitous
animation. Prefer direct manipulation over unnecessary interaction
layers.

Make relationships and state legible: where the user is, what will
happen, what failed, and what to do next. Hide incidental chrome; do not
hide structural truth.

Let interfaces communicate contracts. Correct usage should feel natural;
incorrect assumptions should be hard to make. Design for failure: empty
states, errors, partial progress, and recovery are part of the design,
not afterthoughts.

Do not add "delight" merely to demonstrate design skill. Do not make
users learn a new interaction when an established one works. Do not
remove useful elements merely because they are visually prominent —
completeness matters more than appearance.

Optimize for repeated use, not first-glance impact. The interface should
become natural through use.

## Before you finish

- Would an experienced designer remove or simplify anything that has no
  job?
- Does every visible element earn its attention?
- Does the interaction belong to its platform and category?
- Are important state and failure modes understandable?
- Can users understand it without learning an invented convention?
- Will this still feel right after months of repeated use?

Solution first. Explain at most three deliberate omissions or
refinements when useful.
